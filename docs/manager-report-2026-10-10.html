Cutting the cost of our AI coding assistant

Progress report for management · status at 10 October 2026 · Copilot workflow optimisation project

The goal. Our developers work with an AI coding assistant (GitHub Copilot CLI). It is billed in credits called AIU, and our spending has become large and uneven. This project aims to cut that spending by about half (we expect 30–40%) while keeping the quality of the work the same.

We do it in three steps: measure exactly where the credits go, remove the waste one lever at a time, and check every week, with numbers, that quality has not dropped.

Why it costs so much, in plain words

Before every step, the assistant re-reads the whole conversation so far: on average about 180,000 tokens (≈135,000 words, longer than a typical novel). One re-read is cheap, but a single task takes dozens of steps, so carrying the conversation along adds up to 80% of the bill. Two things make it worse:

  • Pauses. The assistant's short-term memory (the cache) expires after 5 minutes without activity. After a pause the whole history is paid for again, at about 12 times the price. These “cold restarts” are 19% of all spend: resuming a stale session costs ≈64 AIU for its first step, a fresh session only ≈11.
  • Autopilot. In autopilot mode the assistant works alone for many steps in a row. The 91 autopilot sessions are 73% of the spend, and each costs 3.3 times more than a hands-on session.

Bottom line
  • Done: we can now see in detail where the credits go, and the biggest single lever, a normal-size conversation window, has been in force since 9 October (all 14 new sessions since then use it).
  • Not yet proven: the saving itself. The first before/after comparison is possible around 16 October, after one week of data.
  • Needed from management: nothing at this stage. This report is for information.

What exists today

  • A private logbook of every assistant session (a database on the workstation only): 393 sessions (203 of them with cost records), about 1,750 conversation turns, 35,900 tool calls, 20,600 model calls and 223,000 logged events, kept current by a background service. Secrets are masked before anything is stored.
  • A weekly report: spend by model and session type, waiting time, quality indicators.
  • A permission assistant in observation mode since 10 October. It records what it would approve and approves nothing. Each decision takes about 10–14 ms (the limit is 50 ms).
  • Settings changes made on 9 October: normal-size window by default, a one-word switch to the large window, and a per-session spending cap.
  • Tested: 319 automated tests pass; a 19-page plan records decisions, risks and estimates.

What we learned so far

  • The measurements can be trusted. At its first run the logbook reproduced the earlier hand-made baseline almost exactly (129,366 AIU against ≈129.4k; the total has grown since). Row counts match their sources, re-running adds no duplicates, and none of the 2.25 million text fields checked contained a secret after masking.
  • One lever turned out smaller than hoped (a correction). We expected the permission assistant to save 2–4% by removing long waits. Replayed on 4,536 real past command-approval requests, it would have answered 9% of them, and none of the 43 that people refused or cancelled. But those were already the quick ones: together they account for 0.1% of the waiting time and for none of the 118 waits of 5 minutes or more. We therefore do not count on it; the saving has to come from the other levers.
  • Spend is lumpy. A handful of long sessions decide the bill (page 1), so the levers aim at shortening those, not at everyday use.

Quality safeguard

A saving only counts if quality holds. The logbook tracks automatic indicators; today's baseline is 3.0 mid-task stops by a user per 100 messages, 1.3 errors or retries per 100 assistant turns and 276 tasks marked complete. Any lever that makes an indicator more than 25% worse is rolled back.

Cost and expected benefit

Building and testing this system used ≈6.6k AIU in the project's own sessions, about 5% of all recorded spend (the assistant helped to build it). Take the average of ≈12.4k AIU per week, a cautious figure because it includes the three empty August weeks and the weeks with incomplete cost records (the five full weeks since 31 Aug averaged ≈19k). A 30–40% saving is then ≈3.7–5.0k AIU per week, so the build would be repaid in under two weeks once the levers are active. This is a projection: no saving has been measured yet.

Risks and open points

  • Projections, not results. The estimates overlap; the weekly report will show what really happens.
  • Shorter sessions could lose context. Mitigation: automatic hand-over notes and searchable history; the large window stays available on request.
  • Permission assistant. It can only ever say “approved”, and only for a short list of read-only commands inside trusted folders. It never blocks or runs anything, and any error falls back to the normal question. It would be switched on only after a 3-day log review and an explicit human decision, and one setting turns it off again.
  • Data stays local. The logbook and every project tool run on the workstation only; the database accepts connections from this machine alone, nothing is sent to an outside service, and secrets are masked before storage.
  • Open point: the per-session spending cap (1,000 AIU) is missing in about half of the new sessions (6 of 11 real sessions since 9 Oct, test runs excluded). The cause is not yet known.

Glossary

AIUThe credit unit in which GitHub bills Copilot usage.
TokenA piece of text; about three quarters of a word.
Conversation window (context)The history the assistant re-reads before every step.
CacheShort-term memory that makes re-reading cheap. It expires after 5 minutes of inactivity.
AutopilotThe assistant works through a task on its own, for many steps in a row.
Helper agentA second assistant that the main one hands a side task, such as searching the code.
Approval requestThe assistant asking “may I run this command?” before it acts.
